The idea

A living room from 25 cm up

Microduck is a small walking robot designed by Pollen Robotics. I wanted to give it a world: a house full of toys and other ducks that wander, follow you, fetch things and sit down for a rest.

The browser version lets you drive one duck from above and play with the whole flock. The VR version goes one step further and puts you inside the duck, so the sofa becomes a cliff and a football is the size of your head.

Two versions, one duck

Porting a browser toy to a headset

The web game is the behaviour reference. The VR port carries over its gameplay and changes what a headset needs changed. The one feature left behind is the web version's X-ray and sensors view.

Design decisions

Five decisions that shaped the VR game

The view from behind first, the duck's-eye view as a treat

The first VR builds were first person, and first person hides the best part of the game: your own duck waddling, opening its beak and wobbling on skates.

So the main view is now just behind and above the duck. The right stick turns the view, and the duck turns its head to look where you look, so it stays expressive. One click of the left stick drops you to the duck's eye height, where everything is giant. The tutorial teaches that switch as its own little moment.

Comfort first

  • Code never rolls, shakes or pitches the camera, and the horizon is always level. The web version's camera shake was not ported; that energy went into world effects and sound.
  • Smooth turning made me queasy in testing, so turning snaps in 30° steps, with a short vignette pulse.
  • When your duck falls over, the view stays level while the duck tips.
  • Skates are slowed for comfort (about 0.36 m/s, 0.5 when boosting), and seated play is the default.
  • To make you feel small, the whole rig is scaled down, eye separation included. That's what makes a sofa read as a building rather than a big sofa.

A tutorial you play through

The game starts at a mirror, where you build your duck: shell colours, paint per part, a voice. Then 21 short cards walk you through walking, turning, biting, the toy bag, skating, the laser and the duck's-eye view.

Each action card waits until you have really done the thing it asks; only the short talking cards move on by themselves. Holding the menu button skips the lot for people who've played before.

A comic look that survives a headset

Flat toon bands with shadows that lean violet, thick ink outlines, speech and thought bubbles. On a flat screen that's mostly taste. In VR it is also readability: a toy reads as a toy from across the room.

Some of it had to change. Screen-space halftone shimmers in stereo, so the dots now live on the surfaces themselves and fade with distance. Temporal anti-aliasing smears in a headset, so it's gone. The outline pass ports a fix from the web version, where a player reported black bands on Android Firefox.

A world that plays without you

The other ducks run on a small brain with nine behaviours: idle, follow, roam, wander, play with a toy, sit, race, football and chasing the laser. They think out loud in comic bubbles, so you can read what they're up to at a glance.

On top of that: football on the rug, a race through the house, a laser dot the whole flock chases, a toy bag with ducks and toys to drop in, and two-player co-op over the local network for playing with a friend in the same room.

Under the hood

Tools I built to build it

On a solo project, checking your own work is the slow part, so most of my time-savers became small tools.

A scripted player

A virtual player that plays the whole game like a person would: it glances around, snaps its turns, reads each card, misses its first bite and shuffles closer. It filmed the gameplay video above, and running it end to end catches tutorial bugs.

Remote editor control

A small command bridge that can recompile, enter play mode and run checks in Unity from a script, so builds and tests don't need clicking.

Generated art sheets

The design sheets above are rendered by code from the real game assets, so they never drift from what's in the game.

Moves as clips

The duck's walk and moves are computed in code (inverse kinematics, no hand-keyed animation). I record them into Unity animation clips and the GIFs below, for reuse and for documentation.
